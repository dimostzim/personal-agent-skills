# Checklists

Apply only the items relevant to the section's purpose, study type, requested edit, and venue. Flag missing scientific information; never invent it to satisfy a checklist. These are review prompts, not a required template for every paper.

## Abstract Checklist

Reject the abstract if it lacks:

- concrete bottleneck or artifact;
- name and type of introduced resource;
- one-sentence mechanism;
- evaluation scale;
- numerical result when results are available;
- availability information if relevant.

## Introduction Checklist

Reject the introduction if it:

- reads like a review article;
- gives background without connecting it to a decision;
- lists prior methods without explaining their limits;
- does not define the task;
- does not state the intervention clearly;
- ends with generic potential instead of what was done.

## Methods Checklist

Reject the methods if they omit relevant:

- data sources;
- versions or access dates;
- split strategy;
- inputs and outputs;
- thresholds and filters;
- baselines and controls;
- metric definitions;
- statistical tests;
- reproducibility details;
- leakage controls.

## Results Checklist

Reject results if they:

- report numbers without interpretation;
- use only one weak baseline;
- hide a decrease in another metric;
- claim generalization without external or left-out evaluation;
- omit sample size or dataset;
- treat a biased benchmark result as biological evidence;
- use vague phrases like `performed well` without a metric.

## Discussion Checklist

Reject the discussion if it:

- repeats results without synthesis;
- uses boilerplate limitations;
- presents future work unrelated to observed limitations;
- inflates practical impact;
- omits tradeoffs;
- fails to state what remains unresolved.

## Final Paragraph Test

A paragraph should have a clear purpose. Check whichever of these functions it needs, without a quota:

- names a concrete artifact, bias, limitation, dataset, method, or task;
- explains a mechanism;
- justifies a methodological choice;
- reports evidence with a metric, control, or comparison;
- distinguishes related concepts;
- states a tradeoff;
- calibrates the claim.

Rewrite if it:

- could fit almost any biomedical or computational paper;
- describes the field without narrowing to a decision;
- uses promotional adjectives instead of evidence;
- uses flowery metaphors, ornamental transitions, or grand claims in place of a concrete explanation;
- claims improvement without saying over what, where, and by how much;
- ends with vague impact;
- avoids uncertainty where evidence is incomplete.
