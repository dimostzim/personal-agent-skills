# Document Types and Voice

## Thesis or dissertation

The thesis explains a body of research and identifies the student's contribution. Its reader may know the field broadly while being unfamiliar with a particular method, assay or subfield.

**Authorship.** Prefer `I investigated`, `I implemented`, `I compared`, or `I found` for documented individual work, including in the abstract. Use `we` for collaborative actions when the referent is clear, or explicitly state collaboration. Use the method as the subject for its behavior. Avoid repeatedly substituting `this thesis presents` for a direct account of the author's work, but do not start every sentence with `I`.

Read the contribution statement or other supplied authorship evidence before converting team claims to first person. Applying, evaluating, extending and inventing a method are different actions. `I evaluated PHACTn` does not claim development of PHACTn. If ownership is unresolved, preserve the neutral factual statement and flag the attribution question; do not halt unrelated editing.

**Explanation.** Allow foundations, worked examples, derivations, implementation rationale and negative findings enough space to be understood. A background section can teach concepts needed later; every paragraph need not justify an immediate design decision. Define shared concepts once, then use consistent terms and useful cross-references. Avoid both repeated textbook introductions and unexplained compression.

**Organization.** Connect studies through questions, findings and remaining constraints. Describe complementary or parallel contributions honestly; do not turn independent projects into a fictitious sequence of necessary steps. Chapter openings can recall what is established and explain what this chapter investigates. The final discussion should integrate findings across studies rather than reproduce each chapter's summary.

**Abstract.** Establish the research problem and central tasks, identify the author's contributions, and report the findings needed to understand the thesis-level conclusion. A multi-project abstract can give each strand space without becoming a list of paper titles. Follow the institution's actual length requirement; do not impose a one-page rule or journal abstract template.

**Status.** Preserve the distinction between completed experiments, prepared resources and planned research. Stronger prose must not turn a proposed model or integrated system into a completed result. Retain the user's current decisions about progress-stage terminology; do not infer them solely from an old draft.

## Journal or conference research paper

The paper presents a defined scientific contribution to readers in its target field.

**Authorship.** For this user's papers, use `we` for the authors' actions and interpretations, or make the method, experiment, data or result the subject. Do not introduce `I`, including in the abstract, when adapting a thesis passage. Do not mechanically replace every thesis `I` with `we`: check whether the statement belongs to the paper, who did the work, and whether the collective authorship is accurate. Preserve first person inside attributed quotations.

**Explanation.** Include the background needed for the question and interpretation. Define unfamiliar or unusually used terms. Use citations and appropriate supplementary material for detail without omitting information needed to understand or reproduce the main result. Keep the actual comparison and relevant limitations in the paper, even when space is limited.

**Organization.** Follow the target venue and study design. Focus the introduction on this study; remove thesis roadmaps, doctoral-programme narration and unrelated project summaries. A Methods section can have a different order from Results when this improves reproducibility and exposition. Descriptive and claim-based headings are both valid.

**Abstract.** Give the question, intervention or approach, evaluation and main result at the detail appropriate to the venue. Include informative numbers when available. Do not force an artifact, failure mode, mechanistic claim or availability sentence into every abstract. The abstract must agree with the full paper's scope and conclusions.

**Discussion.** Interpret the evidence in relation to the question and relevant prior work. State concrete limits where they affect the conclusion. Do not borrow an essay's certainty or a thesis's longer-term programme as if it were the paper's demonstrated contribution.

## Other scientific text

- **Book chapter:** organize concepts and evidence around the chapter's teaching or synthesis purpose and its place in the book. Its abstract should summarize that scope and the central conclusion; do not automatically use an original-research results format.
- **Review/perspective:** organize concepts and evidence around the question or synthesis. Do not impose an empirical-paper structure or invent a new experiment. Distinguish a proposed framework from a validated account.
- **Proposal/grant:** describe planned work as planned. Explain the question, approach, decision criteria and relevant uncertainty. Use the requested individual or team voice; do not present hoped-for results as observations.
- **Rebuttal:** normally use `we` for the authors. Answer the substantive point, describe the actual change and give its location or evidence. Do not claim a check or experiment was performed unless it was.
- **Caption/table note:** make the scientific content the subject. Explain the comparison, quantities, units, denominators and uncertainty needed to read it; first-person ownership is usually unnecessary.
- **Scientific slides:** preserve scientific distinctions while adapting density to the audience. Use the user's chosen slide format. Presentation text does not inherit the thesis's paragraph length or roadmap.

When adapting between document types, preserve facts and credit while changing scope, explanatory depth, voice and organization together.
