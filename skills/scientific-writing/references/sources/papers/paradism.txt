ParaDISM: Precise mapping of short reads to genes with highly homologous regions

Abstract
Background
Genes with highly similar genomic copies (paralogs, tandem duplications and pseudogenes) pose a major challenge for Short-Read High Throughput Sequencing (srHTS). High sequence similarity makes it difficult to unambiguously identify the sequences of origin of short reads. This results in misalignment artifacts which can propagate through bioinformatic pipelines and increase error rates in subsequent steps such as single nucleotide variant calling. 
Results
We present ParaDISM, a pipeline that refines standard alignments to improve read placement and reduce misalignment-driven false variant calls in highly homologous sequences. The pipeline uses a multiple sequence alignment of reference sequences to identify disambiguating positions and assign a read/read pair to a sequence only when supported by unambiguous sequence-specific evidence. ParaDISM focuses on specificity and precision (positive predictive value) rather than sensitivity by adopting a conservative strategy of read placement and reporting reads with insufficient evidence for the gene of origin as unassigned. An optional iterative refinement procedure calls variants from confidently assigned reads, updates the reference sequences to gain sample-specific evidence for read placement, and re-processes remaining non-assigned reads to mitigate the loss of sensitivity caused by the stringent criteria for read placement. 


We evaluated the performance of ParaDISM both in terms of read alignment and the resulting short variant calls using extensive computational simulation experiments and the Genome in a Bottle HG002 benchmark. We applied ParaDISM to reanalyze two case studies: five public tumour exomes at the GNAQ/GNAQP1 locus, and 18 short-read sequencing datasets of patients diagnosed with Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease (15 exomes and 3 panel sequencing datasets). Compared to Bowtie2, BWA-MEM2 and minimap2, ParaDISM reduced the number of misalignment artifacts, thus increasing specificity and precision of subsequent single nucleotide variant calls.  
Conclusions
ParaDISM refines BAM files to improve the precision of read placement and downstream analyses in highly homologous sequences. The pipeline can be used as a precision-focused add-on to standard bioinformatic pipelines before the variant calling step to provide a stronger level of evidence for variants called in regions of high homology. This approach is useful for applications where false positive variant calls have more severe consequences than false negative ones, such as identifying causal mutations for genetic disorders. In applications where experimental wet-lab confirmation of shortlisted variants is necessary, ParaDISM can be used to prioritise variants with strong support to reduce cost and effort spent on attempting to confirm false positive calls. ParaDISM can be used alongside the results of standard mappers to cross-check results and identify unreliable read placement and variants potentially caused by misalignment artifacts. The pipeline is open source and available under the MIT license at github.com/BioGeMT/ParaDISM.

Background
Short-read High Throughput Sequencing (srHTS) has transformed biomedical research and clinical practice by offering cheap, fast, and massive sequencing capabilities [1,2]. This technology enabled accurate and accessible detection of inherited (germline) variants for diagnosis of genetic disorders, as well as detection and analysis of somatic mutations in cancer for a better treatment and prognosis. However, many medically relevant genes remain either difficult or currently impossible to analyze with srHTS [3–5]. 


One of the major groups of problematic genes are ones that are highly similar to other regions of the genome, such as paralogs (genes originating from an ancestral duplication during the course of evolution), pseudogenes (generally non-functional copies of genes, often former paralogs), tandem duplications, and other highly repetitive regions. High sequence similarity (referred to as sequence homology) makes placement of short reads, and subsequent variant calling, inaccurate [6–11]. This is exacerbated by the design of many alignment algorithms, which either look for the first placement that is “good enough” rather than optimal, or discard reads which have multiple possible placements. As a consequence, misaligned reads contribute to errors in variant calling, resulting in false positives (incorrectly called variants) and false negatives (failure to detect a variant) [6,12].


A prime example of issues caused by homologous sequences is the diagnostics of the Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease (ADPKD). This is one of the most common inherited kidney diseases, with prevalence estimated at nearly 1 in 1000 people worldwide, predominantly caused by mutations in PKD1 and/or PKD2 [13,14]. PKD1 has six pseudogenes with sequence homology up to 97% in some regions [15]. This poses a major challenge for accurate alignment of short reads, which significantly limits the diagnostic applicability of srHTS [16]. Therefore, even though whole-genome sequencing (WGS) can overcome the pseudogene problem in some cases [17,18], and research on optimizing srHTS is ongoing [19], it has not yet replaced long-range PCR followed by Sanger sequencing of nested amplicons as the gold standard [20]. This has important consequences for disease management, excluding disease in kidney donors, and efforts to design treatments such as the recent approaches based on microRNAs [21].


Another example of a possible misalignment artifact reported in the literature involves the GNAQ locus and its pseudogene GNAQP1. Recurrent GNAQ hotspot mutations (p.T96S and p.Y101X) were reported from short‑read WES in natural killer/T cell lymphoma [22]. However, GNAQ shares a short region of very high sequence identity with GNAQP1 that spans the hotspot sites. It was later demonstrated that common GNAQP1 polymorphisms (rs3730150, rs3730148 and rs3730153) can cause short reads to be misaligned by standard aligners and mimic the reported GNAQ hotspot signature [6]. Several tools address paralogous or duplicated regions after read alignment has already been performed. Parascopy and ParascopyVC estimate paralog-specific copy number and perform variant calling in duplicated regions from WGS alignments [23,24], QuicK-mer2 performs mapping-free paralog-specific copy-number analysis from k-mers [25], and CNVnator detects copy-number variation from read depth in BAM files [26]. These approaches address important downstream questions, but they do not modify the BAM files to solve read misalignment. This poses a risk of artifacts propagating through the analysis. 


Some bioinformatic approaches to disambiguate between homologous sequences augment standard short‑read pipelines with locus‑specific steps, often focusing on a single gene or a limited set of duplicates [7,8,27]. For example, PMS2_vaR [27] improves short‑read analysis of PMS2 in the presence of the PMS2CL pseudogene by forcing alignments to the PMS2 reference sequence and using gene‑specific invariant positions to resolve reads from the problematic exon 11 region. Other approaches focus primarily on variant discovery and interpretation in paralogous regions at larger scales. For instance, Chameleolyser [28] reprocesses reads from paralogous loci by realigning them to masked reference sequences and, for indistinguishable paralogs, reports variants as having ambiguous locus assignment. Complementary benchmarking work provides nucleotide‑resolution measures of short‑read sequencing difficulty in exonic regions, e.g., the UNMET score, highlighting positions that are challenging for variant calling with conventional srHTS [29]. Due to the difficulty of accurate read placement, many diagnostic strategies for such genes rely heavily on experimental protocols and specialized laboratory workflows [30,31].


One approach is to use third generation long-read sequencing, which can span regions with less similarity and therefore allow for more accurate read placement [32–36]. However, using long reads is not free from challenges. Long reads do not always fully remove the possibility of misalignment artifacts on extensive regions of high similarity, necessitating the use of specialized software such as DuploMap or Paraphase to improve mapping and variant calling in duplicated regions [37,38]. For example, Paraphase separates haplotypes based on manually identified sites which differ between the reference homologous sequences, e.g. SMN c.840 to separate SMN1 and SMN2. A similar approach based on gene-specific nucleotides is employed by the HSA algorithm to filter VCF files, without modifying the underlying BAM file [39].


A particular limitation of many of the aforementioned approaches is the focus on the genes directly responsible for disease. However, detecting and analyzing variants in homologous regions (such as pseudogenes or paralogs) not only can discover a false positive call in the gene of interest [6], but may also have clinical importance on its own. Pseudogenes have long been considered to lack function, but this view is becoming increasingly challenged [40]. Moreover, there have been documented gene conversions between PKD1 and its pseudogenes [41]. This calls for an approach that can accurately map reads to a gene and all of its homologs to allow for a more accurate variant calling without extensive modifications of laboratory protocols. 


In this work, we present ParaDISM (Paralog Disambiguating Mapper), a pipeline for paralog-aware placement of short reads in highly homologous genomic regions. The goal of the pipeline is to refine BAM files produced by standard aligners such as Bowtie2, BWA-MEM2 or minimap2. ParaDISM takes FASTQ reads and a FASTA reference containing the target sequence together with the homologous reference copies, builds a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) to identify disambiguating sequence-specific positions, uses a standard mapper for initial read placement, and assigns a read or read pair to a reference sequence only when it finds unambiguous evidence. Reads without such evidence are reported separately rather than forced into a copy. Optional iterative refinement can rescue additional reads by incorporating variants called from confidently assigned reads. The pipeline outputs sequence-specific alignments for inspection and downstream variant calling, and can be applied to panel, whole-exome and whole-genome short-read sequencing data. This way, ParaDISM acts upstream of tools such as Parascopy to improve the quality of their input. To our knowledge, this is the first tool to refine short read BAM files produced with standard aligners. 


Implementation 
The ParaDISM pipeline
An overview of the workflow is shown in Fig. 1. ParaDISM is a short-read read-placement refinement pipeline for homologous loci. It takes single-end or paired-end FASTQ reads and a FASTA reference containing the target sequence together with the homologous reference copies to be disambiguated. The reference copies are aligned with MAFFT [42] to define a shared MSA coordinate system, and reads are mapped to the same reference with a selected base aligner. The pipeline outputs per-read or per-read-pair sequence assignments together with sequence-specific FASTQ and BAM files for downstream inspection and analysis. Reads that cannot be placed unambiguously are labeled 'None' and written to a separate FASTQ file.




  

Fig. 1: The ParaDISM pipeline architecture. ParaDISM takes FASTQ reads and a FASTA reference containing the homologous target sequences. The references are aligned with MAFFT to define MSA columns, reads are aligned to the reference FASTA with the selected base aligner, and read bases are then evaluated against the MSA. A read or read pair is assigned to a sequence only when exactly one candidate satisfies C1 (at least n gene-unique anchoring positions; default n=1) and C2 (no conflicting positions). Reads that do not satisfy these criteria are reported as 'None' for separate inspection. Optional iterative refinement calls variants from confidently assigned reads, updates the reference sequences, and reruns the workflow on unresolved reads.


Step 1 (S1 in Fig. 1): Multiple Sequence Alignment.
The reference sequences are aligned using MAFFT [42] to produce a multiple sequence alignment (MSA). The MSA is used to define homology relationships between the nucleotides of all the homologous reference sequences. ParaDISM uses this relation to identify positions that can disambiguate between homologous sequences: MSA columns in which a given sequence has a nucleotide that is different from the homologous nucleotides of all other sequences.


Step 2 (S2 in Fig. 1): Initial Read Alignment.
HTS reads are aligned to the reference sequences using a standard short-read aligner. The current implementation supports Bowtie2 [43], BWA-MEM2 [44], and minimap2 [45], with Bowtie2 as the default. ParaDISM builds the required index for the selected aligner from the provided FASTA reference before alignment, so users do not need to provide pre-built reference indexes. The user can specify a minimum alignment score threshold to filter low-quality mappings and suppress off-target alignments. This initial alignment provides candidate placements and read-to-reference coordinates for the following disambiguation step.


Step 3 (S3 in Fig. 1): Paralog disambiguation.
For each read (or read pair), ParaDISM assigns aligned read bases to MSA columns and evaluates each reference sequence as a candidate gene assignment. For a given candidate, ParaDISM evaluates two conditions:


* Condition 1: Gene-specific positions (S3 C1 in Fig. 1): the read contains at least n distinct bases, where n is user-configurable and defaults to 1, that match the candidate sequence but do not match any other sequence at the homologous MSA column.
* Condition 2: No conflicting positions (S3 C2 in Fig. 1): the read does not contain any base that mismatches the candidate sequence while matching another sequence at the homologous MSA column. 


A read is assigned to a sequence if it is the unique sequence which passes both conditions. ParaDISM does not construct a separate Smith-Waterman scoring matrix for this step. Disambiguating evidence is evaluated from the base-aligner read placement after projecting aligned read bases onto the MSA columns. For paired-end data, evidence is combined across both reads of a paired read (either read can provide an anchoring position, but neither read can introduce a conflict). If no candidate passes, or if multiple candidates pass, the read (read pair) is labeled ‘None’, indicating it could not be assigned to any sequence. We note that only A/C/G/T bases are used in the C1/C2 filters. Gaps in the MSA or in the read alignment are not used as gene-specific positions to avoid conflating gaps in the multiple sequence alignment with indels in the sample, as these gaps have different biological sources: evolutionary relations between reference sequences in the MSA, and sample-specific mutations in read alignments. 


Step 4 (S4 in Fig. 1): Per-gene realignment and output generation.
ParaDISM writes sequence-specific FASTQ files for reads assigned to each sequence and re-aligns those reads to the corresponding sequence (each sequence separately) to obtain within-gene coordinates. The resulting sorted and indexed per-sequence BAM files can be used for visualization and downstream variant calling. Reads not assigned to any sequence by the disambiguating algorithm are reported in a separate FASTQ file and mapped to all reference sequences with a standard mapper to allow for a manual inspection of variants possibly existing in the genome but not supported by reads with unambiguous placement.


Step 5 (S5 in Fig. 1): Iterative refinement (optional).
In iterative mode, ParaDISM attempts to rescue reads initially labeled 'None' by incorporating sample-specific variants into the reference sequences. Genetic variants present in a sample can disrupt anchoring positions and cause otherwise informative reads to be labeled 'None'. ParaDISM calls variants from confidently assigned reads using FreeBayes [46], optionally applies quality filters (QUAL, DP and AF), updates the reference sequences with called variants, and re-runs the workflow on the remaining unassigned reads. Assignments from earlier iterations are preserved, and the procedure stops when no additional reads are reassigned, when no new variants are found, or when the user-defined maximum number of iterations is reached. Users can disable this step if, for a given analysis, they decide that it may pose a risk of accumulation of erroneous assignments due to e.g. incorrect SNV calls. 
Validation on simulated datasets
Reference sequences of PKD1 and its six pseudogenes, PKD1P1-PKD1P6, were downloaded from the Ensembl database (ENSG00000008710, ENSG00000244257, ENSG00000227827, ENSG00000183458, ENSG00000205746, ENSG00000254681, ENSG00000250251; human genome build GRCh38). Sequences of genes located on the negative strand (PKD1, PKD1P4-6) were reverse complemented. The sequences were used as a reference “genome” to generate 1,000 independent paired-end srHTS “panel” simulation replicates with DWGSIM (Whole Genome Simulator for Next-Generation Sequencing; https://github.com/nh13/DWGSIM). Each replicate consisted of 100,000 read pairs (read length 150 bp; insert size 350 ± 35 bp; per‑base sequencing error rate 0.01; SNP rate 0.005; indel rate 0.0005; indel extension probability 0.5). Thus, each replicate contained independently generated sample variants and sequencing reads, and was treated as one simulated patient genome. Random seeds 1–1000 were used to make replicates reproducible. Each replicate was analyzed with ParaDISM with all three supported mappers (10 iterations; FreeBayes quality filters QUAL≥20, DP≥10, AF≥0.05, minimum alternate allele count 5). In the Results section, unless stated otherwise, simulation summaries report means and standard deviations across the 1,000 replicates.
Genome in a Bottle (GIAB) HG002 benchmark
GIAB HG002 (NA24385) Illumina short reads and GIAB/NIST v4.2.1 truth set on GRCh38 were downloaded from the GIAB website (https://www.nist.gov/programs-projects/genome-bottle) [47]. Reads were merged across lanes. In addition to the full dataset, a 10x whole-genome sequencing dataset was generated by downsampling the number of read pairs to 62,746,019 (assuming 500 bp per read pair for 2x250 bp reads). ParaDISM was run with Bowtie2 in local mode for 10 iterations with PKD1 and pseudogenes as the reference FASTA. A minimum Bowtie2 alignment score function of G,60,60 was used to suppress off-target alignments. For 250-bp reads, this setting corresponds to a minimum score of ~391 (approximately 78% of a perfect-match score under Bowtie2's default local scoring). Variants were called using FreeBayes [46]. Truth and callsets were normalized and decomposed into biallelic records; multi-allelic sites were split. Only biallelic SNPs were retained, and evaluation was restricted to GIAB benchmarkable regions. Final benchmark callsets were filtered using INFO/AO ≥ 3, QUAL ≥ 10, INFO/SAF ≥ 1, and INFO/SAR ≥ 1. To evaluate the effect of iterative refinement, variants were also re-called from each available stage-specific BAM against the same original PKD1/PKD1P reference. The intermediate callsets and the final ParaDISM VCF were evaluated using identical normalization, simple biallelic A/C/G/T SNP filtering, the filters listed above, and restriction to the projected GIAB benchmarkable intervals. A true match required identical contig, position, REF, and ALT.


To assess performance beyond PKD1/PKD1P, we applied the same full-depth and 10x HG002 benchmarking workflow to 12 additional homologous gene families, giving 13 families in total. For each family, ParaDISM-refined Bowtie2 alignments were compared with unrefined Bowtie2 alignments using the same variant-calling and evaluation procedure described above.


To obtain locus-specific inputs to measure runtime, we used an indexed, genome-aligned HG002 BAM to extract read pairs overlapping each of the 13 target gene families and 1 kb flanking regions. The extracted reads were converted to paired FASTQ files and supplied to ParaDISM separately for each gene family. This preprocessing was performed outside ParaDISM, which does not accept BAM files as input. For PKD1/PKD1P, we also report an earlier run in which the complete HG002 paired FASTQ files were supplied without locus-based extraction.
GNAQ Case Study
Five whole‑exome tumour datasets (SRR5602384, SRR5602389, SRR5602393, SRR5602414, SRR5602419) were downloaded from NCBI Sequence Read Archive. Reference GRCh38 sequences of GNAQ and its pseudogene GNAQP1 were downloaded from the Ensembl database (ENSG00000156052, ENSG00000214077) and used as a two‑sequence reference FASTA. ParaDISM was run with BWA‑MEM2 for 10 total iterations using a minimum BWA-MEM2 alignment score threshold of 160. Variants were called with FreeBayes using the same filters as in the previous experiments. Read support was summarized using SAMtools [48] by computing per‑base pileup counts at the GNAQ hotspot positions and at the three GNAQP1 SNP positions reported by Lim et al. [6]. 
ADPKD Case Study
Patients with ADPKD were recruited through the nephrology clinics (paediatric and adult) at Mater Dei Hospital, Malta, as part of the Malta NGS Project after written informed consent was obtained (ethics approval number FHS031/2014). The proband in each pedigree had typical ADPKD signs and symptoms as defined by the Barua and Pei criteria [49]. Blood or saliva were collected from 18 patients from 15 different families. Short-read high-throughput sequencing was carried out using either a custom target panel (n=3) or whole exome sequencing (n=15). Libraries for srHTS were prepared as per Agilent SureSelectXT target enrichment protocol for paired-end Illumina sequencing (Agilent SureSelectXT Human All Exon V7). Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina HiSeq4000 platform. Shortlisted potentially causative variants were confirmed using a combination of long-range PCR, nested PCR and Sanger sequencing as previously described [20].


ParaDISM was run with Bowtie2 for 10 total iterations with a minimum Bowtie2 alignment score function of G,40,40. Variants were called using FreeBayes with the same filters as in the previous experiments. To compare ParaDISM with a more standard, non pseudogene-aware approach, FASTQ files were analyzed with NextGENe v.2.4.2.3. In NextGENe, reads were trimmed or rejected when 3 or more bases had a Phred score of 20 or less. Cleaned raw reads were aligned to GRCh37 and variant calls were made (variant minimum coverage 20x, Phred score ⩾Q20, DP≥20, BQ≥20). The resulting VCF files were compared between both approaches. 
Results and Discussion
Precise placement of reads on highly homologous sequences
To evaluate the accuracy of ParaDISM alignment, we generated an extensive dataset of simulated srHTS experiments (where the gene of origin is known for each read pair), and used them to rigorously compare the performance of the proposed pipeline against standard solutions. We generated 1000 paired-end srHTS datasets that simulate panel sequencing of PKD1 and its six pseudogenes, corresponding to a simulated sequencing of 1000 patients. We used the simulated srHTS datasets to evaluate the performance of ParaDISM when applied to refine the BAM files produced by Bowtie2, BWA‑MEM2, and minimap2 (short‑read preset). 


We used three performance metrics to evaluate the alignments before and after ParaDISM refinement: per-sequence sensitivity (the proportion of reads originating from a sequence that were successfully mapped to it), per-sequence specificity (the proportion of reads originating from other sequences that were not mapped to the analyzed sequence), and per-sequence precision (the proportion of reads mapped to the sequence that did originate from it; also known as the positive predictive value). The first two metrics are standard approaches in medical literature and often used to evaluate approaches to variant calling, while the latter is used arguably less often. However, we note that specificity of read placement can be misleadingly high, because the data is inherently unbalanced: more reads originate from other sequences than from any given sequence. This is best illustrated by the following simplified example. Suppose we analyze 10 sequences, with equal coverage of each sequence. Even if the reads are assigned to the sequences completely at random, the expected specificity is equal to 90%. On the other hand, the expected precision in this case is equal to only 10%. Since specificity can be very high even for a completely random assignment, precision is more suitable for a rigorous evaluation of read placement.


On the simulated data, ParaDISM with 10 iterations improved the specificity and precision of read assignment compared to standard aligners (Fig. 2). Averaged across Bowtie2, BWA‑MEM2, and minimap2, specificity increased from 0.97 to 0.99 and precision increased from 0.78 to 0.88. The improvement was consistent across all three aligners, which yielded similar specificity and precision (Fig. 2A), indicating that the choice of aligner used by ParaDISM has a lower impact on the results than the paralog disambiguating step. 


Read assignment matrices (Fig. 2C,D) show that standard aligners produced numerous misalignment artifacts between homologous sequences, whereas ParaDISM reduced these artifacts by concentrating ambiguity in the ‘None’ category. Across 1,000 independent PKD1/PKD1P simulation replicates evaluated with the three initial aligners, mean read-assignment precision increased from 0.78 to 0.88, while sensitivity decreased from 0.78 to 0.63. Depending on the alignment algorithm, ParaDISM assigned 27.7%-30.1% of read pairs to the ‘None’ category, compared with <0.01%-0.6% for the standard aligners without paralog disambiguation. The lower sensitivity reflects ParaDISM’s conservative handling of ambiguity: reads that cannot be placed unambiguously due to excessive sequence homology are labeled ‘None’ instead of forcing an unreliable alignment. The iterative refinement step recovered additional unresolved reads (Fig. 2B). ParaDISM assigned an average of 21,171 read pairs to PKD1, compared with 24,404 for the standard aligners. Despite the lower sensitivity, the number of reads assigned by ParaDISM remained sufficient for downstream analysis.

ParaDISM requires additional computation relative to standard aligners in order to construct the MSA, reassign reads to reference sequences, and perform the final within-sequence alignment. During iterative refinement, the MSA is rebuilt for the updated references, the remaining unassigned reads are realigned and re-evaluated for assignment, and per-sequence BAMs are regenerated from the cumulative assignments. In the simulation benchmark, 10 iterations with 16 workers required 10.5–19.2 min, depending on the initial aligner (Fig. 2E,F). Runtime scaled approximately linearly with the number of iterations, and the PKD1 simulation results stabilized after four to five iterations (Fig. 2B).


ParaDISM has reduced misalignment artifacts between every pair of the analyzed sequences, as evidenced by lower numbers of off-diagonal assignments in read assignment matrices in Fig. 2 C,D. However, the prevalence of misalignment artifacts, and the relative performance in which ParaDISM resolved them, varied highly between the sequences. For both ParaDISM and the standard aligner, most misalignment artifacts occurred between the pseudogene sequences (e.g. out of reads originating from PKD1P1, 351 were placed on PKD1P2 by the standard aligners, compared to only three by ParaDISM; 2038 were placed on PKD1P5 by the standard aligners, compared to 1470 by ParaDISM). Most notably, the standard aligner placed 129 pseudogene-derived read pairs on PKD1, which corresponds to 38700 sequenced bases for 150 read length and represents a major risk for false positive SNV calls. In contrast, ParaDISM incorrectly placed only six reads on PKD1, significantly reducing the risk of these calls. 
To test whether the read-placement results generalize to genes other than PKD1, we repeated the simulation benchmark on 12 additional homologous gene families using the Bowtie2 workflow (Fig. 3). The effect of ParaDISM varied across loci, consistent with differences in sequence homology and the performance of the baseline aligner. Notably, for STRC and alpha-globin (HBA), ParaDISM improved all three metrics (precision, sensitivity and specificity). The iterative refinement procedure allowed ParaDISM to achieve a higher recall than Bowtie2 after the third iteration. For other genes, the increased precision was typically associated with some decrease in sensitivity. For some genes, such as GNAQ/GNA11 and GBA/GBAP1, the baseline alignment was already highly precise, leaving little room for improvement for ParaDISM.


Fig. 3: Read placement performance in simulated experiments on other gene families. ParaDISM-refined Bowtie2 alignments were compared with unrefined Bowtie2 alignments using simulated paired-end reads generated under the same protocol as the PKD1/PKD1P benchmark. Values show mean performance across 100 simulated datasets per gene family.  
We next tested whether sequencing errors or stricter anchoring rules influenced ParaDISM read placement in PKD1/PKD1P simulations (Fig. 4). Across 10 independent simulation replicates per error rate evaluated with Bowtie2, ParaDISM showed an increase in the mean unassigned fraction from 0.172 to 0.648 as the DWGSIM per-base error rate rose from 0% to 5%, while the wrong-assignment rate decreased from 0.098 to 0.035 and sensitivity decreased from 0.731 to 0.317. Overall, mean precision increased from 0.882 at the lowest tested sequencing-error rate to 0.901 at the highest. Thus, within this simulation, increasing sequencing error shifted reads toward unresolved outcomes without increasing cross-paralog assignment. In a separate audit of Bowtie2 iteration-1 evidence from 20 simulation replicates, increasing the minimum unique-support requirement from one to two columns increased mean precision from 0.907 to 0.928 but reduced mean sensitivity from 0.452 to 0.346. The high homology (>97%) of the PKD1/PKD1P sequences means that most short-read pairs overlap at most one gene-specific nucleotide. For the remaining analyses, we required at least one unique-support column. Users can select a higher threshold when a more precision-oriented operating point is appropriate.

Higher precision of variant calling on an established benchmark dataset
As a next evaluation of ParaDISM, we investigated whether the increased specificity and precision of read placement result in more credible variant calling. We used FreeBayes to call SNVs in PKD1 and its pseudogenes using alignments produced by either ParaDISM with Bowtie2 as the initial mapper or Bowtie2 alone on the established Genome In A Bottle (GIAB) HG002 benchmark srHTS dataset with known variants (70x coverage) [47]. 


We compared the performance of SNV calls using sensitivity and precision (also known as the positive predictive value). We note that the aforementioned issue with misleadingly high specificity is even more pronounced in SNV calls than in read placement. A caller which calls SNVs completely at random with probability 1% at each nucleotide has an expected specificity of 99% regardless of the length of the sequence or number of SNVs present. This is because specificity only measures how well a caller identifies sites without variants, and in those sites, this kind of a random caller would make an error with 1% probability. On the other hand, on a sequence length of 1000 bp with 1 SNV present, this kind of a random caller would have a precision of only 0.1%. Because of this, we decided not to compare the specificity of different approaches but use only the precision and sensitivity instead.


On the downsampled 10x dataset, ParaDISM produced 11 true positives and, notably, no false positives, resulting in a precision equal to 1.00 and a 100% reduction in the number of false positive calls (Fig. 5A). On the other hand, while FreeBayes applied to Bowtie2 alignments managed to find 29 true positives, it also produced eight false positives, resulting in a lower precision of 0.78. The reduction in false positive calls thanks to paralog disambiguation was most pronounced in pseudogenes. For example, on PKD1P6, ParaDISM resulted in zero false positives while Bowtie2 produced eight.


On the full 70x dataset, ParaDISM resulted in 5 false positives and 17 true positives compared to 13 false positives and 39 true positives of Bowtie2, corresponding to a moderately increased precision from 0.75 to 0.77 and a 62% reduction in the number of false positive calls (Fig. 5). We note that the number of false positive calls was higher on the 70x dataset than in the 10x one for both ParaDISM and Bowtie2, indicating that, in highly homologous sequences, a higher coverage may pose a higher risk of misalignment artifacts propagating to the SNV calling step, outcompeting the risk of incorrect calls due to sequencing errors in low-coverage datasets. A precise impact of different sources of errors and artifacts in such reference sequences, and their interplay with sequencing depth, remains an open question. We also note that, despite the reduction in the false positive calls, the increase in overall precision due to ParaDISM refinement was moderate in 70x coverage. The likely reason for this is that ParaDISM is limited by the reference sequence homology more than by the coverage, while Bowtie2 is not. Accordingly, the increased coverage allowed Bowtie2 to detect ten more true SNVs (at the cost of reporting five more false SNVs), while ParaDISM detected only six more SNVs (at the cost of reporting also five more false SNVs). 


For the full-depth HG002 analysis, we used a shared Linux server with two Intel Xeon Gold 6342 CPUs. Extracting reads for all 13 gene families from the indexed BAM took 1 min 7 s with 24 threads and yielded 201,114 read pairs. The PKD1/PKD1P subset contained 23,558 read pairs, and its complete workflow took 14 min 54 s with 16 Bowtie2 threads and one read-assignment worker, converging after six iterations. Processing all 13 extracted datasets sequentially took 2 h 21 min 25 s, excluding the shared extraction step. An earlier PKD1/PKD1P run using the complete HG002 paired FASTQs without locus-based extraction took 16 h 33 min 25 s and produced a 463.7-GiB SAM file during the first iteration. 


To quantify the effect of iterative refinement, we evaluated every available stage of ParaDISM in the full-depth and 10x HG002 PKD1/PKD1P analyses. In the full-depth analysis, iteration 1 produced 16 true-positive and four false-positive SNP calls. Iteration 2 retained 16 true positives and contained five false positives, after which the false-positive count remained at five through iterations 3–5 and the final output. The final VCF contained 17 true positives and five false positives. In the 10x analysis, iterations 1–4 contained six true positives and no false positives, while the final VCF contained 11 true positives and no false positives. These analyses did not show progressive accumulation of false-positive SNP calls, although one additional false positive appeared at iteration 2 in the full-depth run. The evidence is limited to PKD1/PKD1P, one sample, and two coverage settings and does not establish stability across other loci, callers, depths, or error profiles. We remark that users can reduce the maximum number of iterations or disable the iterative refinement altogether if they decide that a potential accumulation of errors due to the iterative refinement procedure is a concern, or if an analysis of the results shows that false variant calls were introduced at some iterations. We note that MAPQ filters can potentially be used in the SNV calling step to mitigate the false positive calls, but in our experiments we observed that they have a limited effect on the false positive rate while resulting in a drop in recall, indicating that simple MAPQ filters may not be able to further improve SNV calls from BAM files refined with ParaDISM. We also note that, due to read assignment and within-gene realignment steps in the pipeline, MAPQ scores may not be reliable after BAM refinement, because they only evaluate the uniqueness of read placement within a single gene sequence rather than the whole homologous family.


The lower sensitivity of SNVs called on ParaDISM alignments compared to Bowtie2 was a direct consequence of a more stringent approach to read placement. Accordingly, in the 10x downsampled data set, the false negative calls of ParaDISM were located predominantly in the regions of low coverage, with 32 out of 37 false negative calls located in sites covered with less than 7 reads and only 5 false negative calls located in sites covered with at least 7 reads (Fig. 5). Therefore, the overall decrease in sensitivity from 0.60 to 0.23 can be interpreted as the consequence of conservative read assignment. In practice, it can be mitigated to some extent by inspecting the coverage, either manually or in downstream processing, especially in the context of a targeted analysis of a single gene of interest. Potentially missed variants can also be identified by cross-checking the results of ParaDISM and standard mappers. The user may then decide if the low coverage regions warrant further investigation with other approaches such as PCR or long-read sequencing, or if the detected SNVs are sufficient to answer the research question at hand. In this context, we emphasize that ParaDISM is not designed to give a comprehensive overview of SNVs in a sample, but rather to reduce the number of high-confidence false positive calls made by standard aligners by indicating whether a given dataset of short reads provides sufficient evidence to support the presence of an SNV. This approach is analogous to the logic of statistical hypothesis testing, where a small p-value indicates evidence for the alternative hypothesis, while a high p-value indicates insufficient evidence to make a conclusive decision. By this analogy, sufficient coverage in ParaDISM indicates evidence for the presence of an SNV (similarly to low p-values), while lack of SNV calls indicates that a given dataset of reads provides insufficient evidence to make a conclusion (similarly to high p-values).

GNAQ case study: ParaDISM mitigates misalignment artifacts
To illustrate how ParaDISM can protect against misalignment artifacts, we applied it to re-analyze SNVs in GNAQ and GNAQP1 sequences in five tumour WES data sets in which previous reports suggested the possibility of false SNVs caused by misalignment artifacts [6]. In the SNVs called by FreeBayes from ParaDISM alignments, no alternative base on GNAQ was observed at either hotspot position originally reported by Li et al. (2019) across the cohort (GNAQ:206083 ALT=0/200; GNAQ:206100 ALT=0/284; Table 1). At the three GNAQP1 SNP positions described by Lim et al. (2022), the full T,C,T ALT triplet was not observed: the rs3730150 ALT base (T) was present at low frequency at GNAQP1:765 (13/153 reads), whereas the rs3730148 ALT base (C) at GNAQP1:786 and the rs3730153 ALT base (T) at GNAQP1:826 were not observed (0/162 and 0/237 reads, respectively; Table 1).


These results illustrate how ParaDISM is less prone to align reads in ambiguous cases compared to standard aligners. In this case study, the pipeline did not provide a conclusive answer as to whether the hotspots are genuine variants or misalignment artifacts, indicating that this question may warrant further studies. The result of ParaDISM should not be interpreted as a confirmation that the reported variant is a false positive, but rather as an indication of insufficient evidence to make a conclusive decision, in agreement with conflicting reports in the literature. This way, ParaDISM avoids the “confident uncertainty” that may affect the standard aligners and cause them to output misaligned reads with a high MAPQ score, which can result in confidently wrong SNV calls. The latter can happen especially when, due to the presence of variants or gene conversion, the incorrect location has a higher alignment score than the correct one (as suggested in the case of GNAQ [6]). Since ParaDISM does not rely on alignment score alone, its results are less affected by a potentially misleadingly high MAPQ.

ADPKD case study: ParaDISM detects medically relevant variants
In the final case study, we demonstrate that, despite the lower sensitivity, ParaDISM can identify medically relevant variants. We applied ParaDISM to analyze srHTS data of patients diagnosed with ADPKD (three panel, 15 WES datasets) and compared it to the variant outputs of a more standard approach that is not paralog-aware. 


The NextGENe v.2.4.2.3 (SoftGenetics, USA) pipeline reported potentially causative variants in 13 of the 15 ADPKD families: a splicing mutation in PKD2 and 14 shortlisted variants in PKD1 which included 5 truncating SNVs, 8 missense variants and 1 in-frame 3 bp deletion. No variants were shortlisted in 2 families.


Analysis of the same datasets using ParaDISM confirmed 5 truncating variants, 5 missense variants and the in-frame deletion variant. Furthermore, ParaDISM did not call 2 missense variants reported by the NextGENe pipeline (p.C508R in exon 7 and p.F3873V in exon 42) which alternative sequencing methods had shown to be absent from PKD1 despite prior shortlisting from srHTS datasets, and were likely caused by misalignment artifacts. Finally, PKD1 p.R739Q, a commonly reported benign variant which had been called in a number of patient datasets, was not called by ParaDISM. This variant is currently labelled as a low quality variant in the latest gnomAD database (v4.1.0). On the other hand, a potentially causative missense variant confirmed to be on exon 11 of PKD1 by long-range PCR and Sanger sequencing of nested PCR product was not detected by ParaDISM. This variant was located in a region with a low coverage of ParaDISM alignments. The reads bearing this variant were identified in the BAM file containing reads that did not pass the paralog disambiguating conditions. 
Conclusions
Highly similar genomic sequences are a major challenge for short-read high-throughput sequencing. Standard pipelines that force alignments in these regions can misplace reads, and the resulting misalignment artifacts can propagate through pipelines and result in false positive and false negative variant calls [50]. The relative consequences of these errors depend on the application, and no single precision–sensitivity balance is uniformly optimal for all use cases. In applications where false positives can be more adverse than false negatives, increasing placement stringency can improve precision at the cost of sensitivity.  


ParaDISM is optimized for precision rather than sensitivity. It adopts a conservative approach to read placement, explicitly reporting unresolved ambiguity rather than masking it as a confident alignment.  This approach is particularly useful for those diagnostic settings in which false positives can be more harmful than reduced sensitivity. This is often the case in clinical settings when high-throughput sequencing is used to confirm a clinical diagnosis and identify causal mutations. In these cases, a false negative often does not mean the end of genetic testing, but rather the need for further testing, possibly with different techniques. This is especially the case when patients are diagnosed with orthogonal methods, such as kidney ultrasonography in the case of ADPKD. In this context, a false positive result carries a substantial risk of wrong diagnosis by misidentifying the causal factor for the disease. In some cases, this may result in the patient receiving the wrong treatment while not receiving the correct one. In other cases (like ADPKD), it may lead to adverse outcomes when using targeted methods to check for the presence of a supposedly causal mutation in family members, who could benefit by early treatment before the onset of symptoms. A false positive in a patient may result in targeted tests for a mutation that is absent in the family members, while not searching for the true causal mutation. Another context in which false positives pose a substantial harm is population or neonatal screening programmes. False positives limit their usefulness and overall benefit by subjecting healthy individuals to unnecessary treatments and posing adverse psychosocial consequences [51,52]. The vast number of tests performed in screening programmes, as well as the ability to perform multiple tests with the modern omics technologies, mean that even a small false positive rate can result in numerous incorrect diagnoses, resulting in overdiagnosis and overtreatment, both at the level of individuals and populations [53–55].


To mitigate the rate of false positives, ParaDISM can be used as a precision-enhancing add-on to variant-calling workflows or used in parallel with standard approaches. When used in combination with standard variant calling pipelines, it can increase the level of confidence in called variants in regions of high sequence homology. Variants detected by standard mappers but not confirmed by ParaDISM can be flagged for scrutiny either by experimental or computational methods. In particular, ParaDISM allows the users to inspect whether these unconfirmed variants on the gene of interest can now be found on its pseudogenes, in which case they can be considered as false positives caused by misalignment artifacts of standard mappers. Variants called by standard mappers from reads unmapped by ParaDISM (i.e. absent from any pseudogene or homologous region) can be tested further to confirm their presence and location with experimental methods such as long-range PCR and Sanger sequencing or long-read sequencing in case of clinically important loci. This way, ParaDISM can be used as a confirmatory tool to strengthen the evidence of shortlisted variants. In applications when shortlisted variants need to be confirmed experimentally, this approach can help to avoid unnecessary wet-lab experimental cost and effort spent on analysing false positive calls. This way, ParaDISM can complement, rather than replace, other workflows.


As demonstrated by the case studies, ParaDISM can be used with different types of srHTS data: panel sequencing (e.g. the PKD1 case studies), whole-exome sequencing (e.g. the GNAQ and PKD1 case studies) and whole-genome sequencing (e.g. the GIAB benchmark). We remark that since the runtime increases with input size, locus-specific extraction may be needed for whole-exome and whole-genome data. When an indexed, genome-aligned BAM is available (produced by standard mappers), users can reduce runtime and memory requirements by extracting read pairs that overlap the target gene family and their flanking regions before running ParaDISM. We used this external preprocessing step for the locus-specific runtime analyses. Alternatively, users may use a standard aligner to first align reads to the target gene family to filter out reads from other loci. ParaDISM does not currently perform this extraction by default, so users must convert the extracted reads to paired FASTQ files before analysis. We also remark that while the pipeline requires users to provide the specific sequences of interest and can only analyze a single set of reference sequences at a time, a single srHTS data set can be reused to study multiple genes by running ParaDISM with different sets of reference sequences.


We remark that ParaDISM’s performance may be sensitive to the quality of the multiple sequence alignment. Algorithms for multiple sequence alignment are continuously improving, but for some sequences, obtaining an accurate MSA is still a challenging task. Therefore, while ParaDISM performs the MSA automatically using MAFFT, experienced users may want to inspect the quality of the MSA to identify potential issues with ParaDISM refinement. Furthermore, we note that ParaDISM also depends on the quality of the reference sequences provided by the user, and incorrect or incomplete references may limit its ability to refine the read alignments.  


Future work can further improve the performance of ParaDISM. Currently, while the software tackles disambiguation between duplicated genes, it does not explicitly disambiguate alternative placements within duplicated regions of a single gene. Instead, it evaluates the alignment records reported in the input SAM and assigns a read pair only when those records provide unique support for exactly one gene. Furthermore, if a patient has variants in the anchoring sites, especially resulting from gene conversion and thus matching other sequences, ParaDISM may not be able to place reads around them. Finally, ParaDISM may potentially produce false negative calls in heterozygous variants if reads from only one haplotype can be placed confidently. Cross-checking with other bioinformatic pipelines or inspecting the unassigned reads is recommended to detect potential SNVs in reads that did not pass the paralog disambiguating criteria.
