# Sutton: evidence for reusable writing rules

Research date: 2026-10-04. These are stylistic observations from primary texts, not claims that Sutton explicitly prescribes these rules. The sample contains essays and exposition, not a representative sample of empirical Results or Methods sections. Author-specific attribution is weaker for coauthored texts.

## Sources and observations

### Richard Sutton, The Bitter Lesson (2019)

Source: http://incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html . Read the whole essay. Paragraph locations count prose paragraphs after the date.

- Paragraph 1 states the central claim immediately, explains the proposed reason, names the competing approach, then previews examples.
- Paragraphs 2–5 use chess, Go, speech recognition, and computer vision to instantiate the same proposed pattern. Repeating the comparison makes the argument easy to follow.
- Paragraph 6 compresses the examples into four explicit propositions; paragraphs 7–8 draw two implications. The conclusion returns to the opening issue.
- Sentence lengths vary. Short statements punctuate longer explanations and qualifications; the style is not simply short sentences throughout.
- Limitation as a style model: this is a polemical essay. Sweeping claims and emotional judgments should not be imported into empirical manuscripts. Directness does not establish evidential support.

### Richard S. Sutton, John McCarthy’s Definition of Intelligence (2020)

Source: http://incompleteideas.net/papers/Sutton-JAGI-2020.pdf . Read printed pp. 66–67, the first two PDF pages. The file subsequently includes the special issue; those other articles are not Sutton's text.

- Page 66 begins with a concrete disagreement and why it matters, then introduces the definition under discussion.
- Consecutive paragraphs unpack separate components: intelligence as an ability, its computational part, and achieving goals. Each paragraph adds a specific distinction.
- Pages 66–67 develop one thermostat example by changing the observer and keeping the system fixed. A chess example then checks the distinction in another setting.
- The last paragraph combines the preceding distinctions into a compact definition.
- Terms recur: goal, system, observer, outcome, mechanism. Repetition preserves the argument's objects.
- First-person statements identify Sutton's judgment as his judgment. Conversational questions help exposition, but need not become a manuscript template.

### David Silver and Richard S. Sutton, Welcome to the Era of Experience

Source: https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/Era-of-Experience%20/The%20Era%20of%20Experience%20Paper.pdf . Read pp. 1–8; particularly Streams (pp. 2–3), Rewards (pp. 3–4), Planning and Reasoning (p. 5), and the conclusion (p. 8). This is a coauthored book-chapter preprint.

- Named sections divide the proposal into explicit components. Health, education, and science examples recur, so each new component extends a familiar scenario.
- The Rewards discussion connects the abstract claim to quantities that could be observed; p. 4 supplies a candidate reward mechanism and acknowledges other possible approaches.
- Footnote 2 (p. 4) prevents a false dichotomy by noting that human interaction can be part of experience.
- This is an imperfect vocabulary model for the user's preference: the text contains promotional language, including “unlock,” “pave the way,” and “unprecedented.” Its predictions should not be copied as the default degree of certainty for scientific results.

## Recommended rules, adapted to the user

These are editorial recommendations inferred from the passages above and constrained by the user's stated preferences; they are not quotations or instructions attributed to Sutton.

1. Put the actual claim, question, or definition near the start. Supply only the background needed to understand it.
2. Explain the reasoning in dependency order. Define a concept before using it to justify another claim; give each paragraph a specific explanatory job.
3. When an abstraction is difficult, work through a concrete example. Change one relevant condition at a time so the distinction is visible.
4. Keep the same name for the same thing. Repeat technical terms instead of varying them for elegance.
5. Say what causes what, what was measured, and how the evidence supports the claim. Replace claims of importance with the relevant mechanism or result.
6. Use short sentences for emphasis when useful, and longer sentences when conditions or qualifications require them. No mechanical word or sentence-length limit.
7. End a passage by resolving its question or stating what follows. Do not attach generic praise or future impact to every paragraph.
8. Preserve scientific evidence standards: distinguish measured results, interpretation, and conjecture; retain conditions and limitations. Do not inherit an essay's rhetorical certainty.

A useful selective model is the JAGI note's progressive clarification and concrete examples. “Write like Sutton” by itself is underspecified and can also reproduce the rhetoric the user dislikes.
